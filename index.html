National Overview

Wages and Unemployment:
A Thirty-Year Divergence

When unemployment is high, workers lose bargaining power and wages stagnate. When unemployment falls, wages should recover. But since the GFC, that relationship has broken down - unemployment fell steadily from 2012 to 2019, yet wages barely moved. This "decoupling" is the central labour market puzzle of modern Australia.

Unemployment rate Year-ended wage growth GFC period COVID period
Key insight: Wage growth tracked closely with unemployment movements through the 1990s and early 2000s. After the GFC, unemployment fell from its 2012 peak of ~5.8% down to 5% by 2019 yet wages growth flatlined near 2%, well below its historical 3–4% range. The relationship broke.
Sources: RBA Table H5 (Labour Force, ABS) · RBA Table H4 (Wage Price Index, ABS) · 1999–2025

Structural Labour Market Change

The Casualisation of Work:
Full-time vs Part-time Employment

One reason wages have stagnated is structural: the workforce has shifted toward part-time and casual employment. Part-time workers typically earn less, have fewer entitlements, and wield less bargaining power. As the part-time share of employment has grown, the average worker's leverage has declined. Drag the slider to watch the shift happen year by year.

Key insight: Part-time employment has grown faster than full-time in nearly every period since 1997. The composition of the workforce has materially shifted more Australians are now in jobs with reduced hours, lower earnings, and less security.
Source: RBA Table H5 (ABS Labour Force Survey) · 1997–2025

What does Australia's workforce look like in any given year?

Drag the slider to move through time from 1999 to 2025. Watch the gold part-time slice grow steadily as full-time employment shrinks as a share this is casualisation happening in real time.

By 2025, nearly one in three employed Australians works part-time. In 1999 it was closer to one in four.

Wage Stagnation

Wages vs Productivity:
Where Did the Gains Go?

In a well-functioning labour market, productivity improvements translate into wage increases over time. Workers become more efficient, firms earn more, and that surplus is shared. Australia's labour market tells a different story: productivity has continued to grow, but wage growth has consistently lagged behind since the mid-2000s.

Productivity growth (YoY %) Wage growth (YoY %) Private sector wages
Key insight: The bars between the two lines represent the wage-productivity gap. Red bars indicate periods where wages outpaced productivity rare and brief. Gold bars show when productivity led the dominant condition since 2012. The gap represents value captured by capital rather than labour.
Sources: RBA Table H4 Wage Price Index & Non-farm Labour Productivity (ABS) · 1997–2025
Key insight: This chart pairs unemployment with job vacancies as a percentage of the labour force. A rising vacancy ratio alongside persistent unemployment signals structural mismatch employers cannot find workers with the right skills, while the unemployed cannot access the available roles. Post-2021, this pattern became acute.
Sources: RBA Table H5 - Unemployment Rate & Job Vacancies to Labour Force Ratio (ABS) · 1997–2025

Regional Inequality

Not All Australians Feel
the Same Labour Market

National averages mask enormous regional variation. Unemployment in some Sydney suburbs exceeds 15,000 persons, while affluent beachside areas record fewer than 5,000. The geographic concentration of unemployment is not random it tracks closely with socioeconomic disadvantage, distance from major employment centres, and industrial composition.

Key insight: The choropleth reveals a strong urban–rural and inner–outer metropolitan gradient. Melbourne's west records the highest absolute unemployed count at 36,100 persons followed closely by Sydney's outer south-west. Remote and regional areas appear lighter because their populations are smaller, not because unemployment is lower.
Source: ABS Labour Force Detailed, Table MRM1 SA4 Modelled Estimates (most recent available month)
Key insight: State-level aggregation from SA4 data shows NSW and VIC dominate in absolute unemployment numbers due to population size. However, trend movements reveal how smaller states like South Australia have experienced persistently elevated unemployment relative to their workforce size. Hover a state name in the legend to isolate it.
Derived: SA4 codes aggregated to state by prefix digit. Source: ABS SA4 Modelled Estimates · 2012–2025

Economic Shocks & Full-Cycle View

COVID, Recovery, and the
Labour Market's Long Memory

The COVID-19 pandemic delivered Australia's largest peacetime labour market shock. Unemployment surged to 7.5% in July 2020 levels not seen since the early 1990s. The subsequent recovery was unusually rapid, but the shock revealed long-standing structural vulnerabilities that persist to this day.

Key insight: Unemployment and participation rate moved in opposite directions during the initial COVID shock many workers left the labour force entirely (discouraged workers), masking the true depth of job loss. The rapid recovery in both metrics from late 2020 is historically unusual.
Source: RBA Table H5 ABS Labour Force Survey · 2018–2023
Key insight: The heatmap normalises each indicator to its own mean (z-score), allowing direct comparison across metrics with different scales. Red cells indicate elevated stress (above-average unemployment, below-average hours and growth). The GFC (2008–2010) and COVID (2020) stand out clearly as the two most stressed periods in the dataset. The 2013–2019 stagnation period shows persistent mild stress across all indicators.
Derived: z-score normalisation per indicator. Unemployment inverted so red = stress throughout. Source: RBA H5

About this visualisation

This visualisation examines the Australian labour market between 1997 and 2025, focusing on whether wage growth has kept pace with employment conditions, how the composition of work has shifted, and where regional inequality is most acute. It is designed for a general Australian audience with no assumed statistical knowledge. The topic is of personal interest given its direct relevance to household living standards and economic opportunity.

Generative AI (Claude by Anthropic) was used in the development of this visualisation to assist with Vega-Lite JSON specification structure, HTML and CSS layout code, and grammar review of narrative text. All analytical decisions, data selection, visualisation design choices, and storytelling were developed by the author. Data was sourced exclusively from the RBA and ABS.
